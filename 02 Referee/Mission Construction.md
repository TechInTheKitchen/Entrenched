# Mission Construction

Use [[Enemies and Monsters]] for mobile opposition, [[Stationary Defenses]] for puzzle-like emplacements, and [[Retaliation Tables]] for battlefield consequences.

An adventure of **Entrenched** follows one operation across a persistent map. The referee prepares most of no-man's-land before play, then changes enemy activity in response to what the sappers discover, destroy, and reveal.

The map is not a sequence of disposable encounters. It is hostile ground the players learn over repeated deaths. A good operation becomes more understandable with every run even as the enemy becomes more aware of the unit.

## Operation Map

A standard operation map is:

- **36 squares wide**
- **84 to 144 squares long**
- Anchored by the Allied trench along one short edge
- Oriented toward the objective and enemy line near the opposite edge

The precise physical distance represented by a square matters less than consistency. Combat uses the range and movement rules in [[Skill Checks and Combat]].

Longer maps are not automatically harder. A 144-square map should contain more useful routes, shelter, and discoveries rather than merely requiring more movement rolls.

Do not count every square during ordinary exploration. Advance the unit between meaningful locations and decisions. Use exact movement only during combat, pursuit, timed hazards, or dangerous crossings where position and exposure matter. Once the unit proves a route safe, let later runs cross it quickly until the battlefield changes.

## Preparing the Ground

For a fast physical setup, use a roll of butcher paper, kraft paper, the blank back of wrapping paper, or taped one-inch grid sheets. Give yourself ten minutes to establish the trenches, major landmarks, broken routes, and broad danger areas. When the timer ends, stop making the map neat. Add hidden obstacles and enemy positions in rough notation, then let smaller findings enter the map as the unit explores. The battlefield should look used, corrected, and argued over rather than professionally drafted.

A full 36-by-84 or 36-by-144 field is intentionally long and works best on a paper roll laid across or beside the table. If space is limited, divide the same map into connected sections such as **Approach**, **Killing Ground**, and **Enemy Works**. Keep coordinates or matching edge marks so routes continue cleanly between sheets. This changes only how the map is handled, not its total length.

Before play, draw or place the features that will remain stable throughout the operation:

- Ridges, depressions, mud, flooded ground, and shell craters
- Burnt-out tanks and other artillery shelter
- Ruined homes, bunkers, chapels, factories, and farm structures
- Barbed wire, tank traps, barricades, and fortified walls
- Known and hidden tunnel entrances
- Minefields and other buried hazards
- Enemy trench lines and permanent fortifications
- Several possible approaches to the objective

Build pillboxes, projectors, and other fixed weapons using [[Stationary Defenses]]. Their coverage and triggers belong on the referee's map. Give every emplacement visible evidence, blind ground, and several ways to create an opening rather than treating it as an enemy with a larger Defense.

Then prepare mobile or reactive elements:

- Patrol routes and patrol composition
- Sentries and firing positions
- Artillery and mortar targets
- Corpse recovery routes
- Enemy reserves
- Traps or ambushes the enemy may deploy after learning the sappers' habits

The referee's map contains all prepared information. The players begin with a clean copy showing the Allied trench, broad landmarks known to command, and the approximate objective when its location is known.

## Recommended Supplies and Battlefield Salvage

Before the first briefing, prepare a short **recommended supplies** list based on what command believes about the operation. Give it to the players before outfitting. It should suggest useful categories without revealing the correct route or every hidden threat.

A useful list contains five to eight entries drawn from:

- A weapon suited to the expected engagement range
- A tool for the most obvious barrier or terrain
- A way to locate a hidden threat
- Explosives suited to the known objective
- Smoke, camouflage, or another way to break sight
- Field dressings or revival equipment
- One item command recommends for the wrong reason
- One scarce item the unit is told may be recoverable in the field

The list is advice, not extra equipment. Sappers still obey their slots, rank access, and assigned-bearer rules.

Seed two to four salvage opportunities on the map: an abandoned Allied cache, a dead earlier unit, a wrecked supply carrier, enemy equipment, or a tool trapped behind the obstacle it could solve. At least one find should answer a danger on the recommended list, and at least one should offer an unexpected alternative. Mark recovered equipment on the shared map. Unless an operation explicitly says an item can be claimed immediately, it must reach the Allied trench and be formally issued during outfitting before it grants its mechanical benefit.

Salvage should reward exploration without becoming a general inventory hunt. Finding the right tool can reduce an operation-level Resistance, open a route, restore a scarce supply, or change what the unit carries on the next run.

## Layers of No-Man's-Land

A useful map has three loose layers. These are design guidelines rather than visible boundaries.

### The Approach

The ground nearest the Allied trench teaches the operation's basic dangers. It should contain several routes, early shelter, and clues pointing toward deeper threats. Repeatedly crossing it becomes faster once the sappers mark safe ground.

### The Killing Ground

The center of the map contains overlapping hazards, patrols, exposed lanes, ruins, and competing routes. Discoveries here should create meaningful choices: speed against safety, shelter against enemy occupation, or a direct route against a hidden one.

### The Enemy Works

The far end contains the strongest defenses and the mission objective. It should reward information or sabotage gathered during earlier runs. A frontal attack may be possible, but the map should hold at least two ways to weaken, bypass, isolate, or surprise the final position.

## What Remains Between Runs

The operation map is persistent. Unless a specific event changes them, the following remain where the players marked them:

- Terrain and substantial shelter
- Cleared or confirmed minefields
- Destroyed wire and barriers
- Collapsed or opened tunnels
- Destroyed machines and fortifications
- Placed traps and abandoned equipment
- Discovered routes and landmarks

The following may change between runs:

- Bodies may be recovered, consumed, rebuilt, or moved
- Patrol personnel may be replaced
- Patrol timing may change while the route remains recognizable
- Enemy sappers may shift firing positions
- Reserves may reinforce a damaged area
- New mines or traps may appear where the unit has become predictable

Do not erase useful information arbitrarily. Changes should follow from enemy knowledge, visible battlefield events, or the operation's established threats. When a known feature changes, let the players cross out, revise, or annotate their own map. Its accumulated corrections are part of play.

## Enemy Reinforcement

Killing a patrol matters during the current run, but enemy manpower is vast. Unless the unit cuts off reserves, replacements may occupy the same route before the next run.

Reinforcement should preserve learned patterns without making every run identical. A marked patrol route remains valuable for timing an ambush or placing a trap, even if the replacement patrol is larger, earlier, or accompanied by a corpse hound.

Permanent victories require action against the system supporting the enemy. Destroying a barracks, collapsing an access trench, poisoning a corpse depot, or severing communications may remove or reduce reinforcements for the rest of the operation.

## The Objective

The players usually know the objective's approximate location from their briefing. Command may identify an enemy trench sector, a ruined village, a battery somewhere beyond a ridge, or a suspected tunnel complex without possessing an accurate position.

Some missions begin with no reliable location. In those operations, early runs are reconnaissance. Clues, captured documents, patrol movements, sound, and terrain gradually narrow the search.

An objective should require several discoveries or changes to the map before it becomes reasonably achievable. Avoid locking the operation behind one indispensable check. Failed checks bring retaliation, but another route, another run, or a more violent solution should remain possible.

Treat Resistance 4 or higher as an operation problem rather than a larger single roll. Prepared discoveries and sub-objectives reduce its Resistance or remove prerequisites. A bunker might begin at Resistance 4 and be impossible to breach directly; cutting its reinforcement tunnel reduces it to 3, and detonating its ammunition cache reduces it to 2.

## Reactive Enemy Doctrine

After each run, consider what the enemy learned:

1. Where were the sappers seen?
2. Which route did they use?
3. What did they destroy, steal, or leave behind?
4. Which enemy positions survived to report?
5. Did the enemy recover an Allied body or piece of equipment?

Choose one or two logical responses. Move a firing position, reinforce a patrol, mine a discovered route, use a corpse as bait, or pull forces away from another part of the map. Responses should create new decisions rather than simply make the best-known route unusable.

If no enemy survives or observes the unit's actions, the enemy should not respond with impossible knowledge.

## Pacing a Run

A run should take about thirty minutes of real time. Keep attention on immediate choices:

- Which route does the unit take?
- What does it stop to investigate or mark?
- Who risks a check?
- Does the unit press forward, revive the dead, or retreat?

Call for checks only when failure can provoke meaningful retaliation. Once the players understand a safe route, let them cross familiar ground quickly until something has changed.

If discussion stalls, signal an approaching patrol, descending shell, engine noise, or other immediate danger. Ask for a decision. If the unit still hesitates, retaliate as described in [[Skill Checks and Combat]].

# Mission Frameworks

Each framework supplies an operation's final objective and the kinds of discoveries that make it possible. Any framework can be reskinned for a different stretch of the front.

## 1. Raise the Red Flag

**Objective:** Capture an enemy trench section and raise the Allied signal flag so reinforcements can advance.

The unit must find a viable crossing, breach the outer defenses, silence the trench's weapons, and hold the position long enough to raise the flag. The approximate location is known from the beginning.

Useful discoveries include a buried approach, an observation blind spot, a weak section of wire, a reserve route, or the position of the trench's signal bunker.

The enemy reacts by strengthening whichever approach the sappers reveal. Permanent progress comes from destroying defensive structures and cutting reinforcement trenches.

## 2. Silence the Battery

**Objective:** Locate and destroy an artillery battery shelling the Allied line.

Command knows the direction of fire but not the battery's exact location. The unit must follow sound, shell trajectories, ammunition carriers, or communication wire to find it.

The guns may occupy armored pits, travel on rails, or be integrated into a living fortification. Destroying ammunition, severing observation lines, and trapping resupply routes can weaken the final assault.

The enemy may move individual guns or establish false firing positions after each attack.

## 3. Cut the Buried Nerve

**Objective:** Enter an underground communication complex and sever the enemy's control of the sector.

The surface map conceals a second network of tunnels. The unit must locate entrances, determine which passages connect, and identify the command or signal organ at the center.

Collapsing the wrong passage may close a route while opening another. Enemy sappers can emerge behind the party through unmarked shafts. Once the buried nerve is cut, patrol reinforcement and coordinated artillery cease for the remainder of the operation.

## 4. Recover the Black Case

**Objective:** Find and return an intelligence case, prototype, officer, or preserved body lost in no-man's-land.

Command knows the missing unit's planned route but not where it fell. The players reconstruct its final movements from bodies, spent equipment, map scraps, and enemy recovery activity.

The target must be carried back to the Allied trench, turning the return journey into part of the mission. The enemy may move it between runs or use it as bait once it recognizes the search.

## 5. Burn the Battleborn

**Objective:** Permanently destroy an enemy Battleborn that repeatedly returns to command local patrols.

Killing the target is insufficient. The unit must learn its routes, locate the remains it uses for repair, defeat it, and incinerate or otherwise annihilate the body.

Each failed attempt teaches the creature about the sappers. It may adopt their tactics, use parts recovered from their bodies, and remember individual characters across revivals.

The mission should offer several means of permanent destruction, including carried incendiaries and dangerous environmental opportunities.

## 6. Collapse the Iron Saint

**Objective:** Destroy a mobile fortress, siege engine, or enormous reconstructed war organism before it reaches the Allied trench.

Its expected path crosses the long map, providing an approximate location that changes as the operation continues. A direct attack is nearly impossible. The unit must discover weak points and prepare the ground.

Possible solutions include mining a bridge, opening flooded ground, sabotaging its fuel or circulation, killing its guidance crew, or luring it over buried charges. The target advances or changes position between runs, making time visible on the shared map.

## Completing the Operation

Mission success should be unmistakable. The flag rises, the guns stop, the buried signals go silent, the case reaches Allied hands, the Battleborn burns, or the Iron Saint collapses.

Once the objective is complete, the adventure ends even if the war does not improve. Command records the surviving sappers, gathers whatever equipment remains, and prepares the next unit for another part of the front.
