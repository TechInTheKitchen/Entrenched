# Changing Ground

Use this referee procedure when the Front opens a route, exposes a structure, or changes terrain the unit already knows. [[The Shifting Front]] holds the setting and sensory context; this page keeps the map and movement rules together.

## Traveling by Hollow

Telegraph underground dangers as honestly as surface fire. The horror should come from choosing whether the protected route is worth what the signs imply, not from an unseen collapse imposed without warning.

A known hollow lets the unit bypass surface sight, wire, and ordinary firing lanes while it remains intact and unoccupied. Mark every confirmed entrance, branch, obstruction, and exit on the shared map. Draw the hollow as a dotted route or as a small inset beneath the surface map so players can see which landmarks it connects without needing a complete floor plan.

Moving through a mapped, stable hollow requires no check. Use Resistance 0 to study an entrance safely, sound the nearby ground, compare buried walls with the surface, or determine what additional route might connect to it. Each success can reveal an exit direction, load-bearing wall, dry chamber, airflow, recent passage, salvage, or the location of danger without requiring the sapper to enter it.

Entry becomes Resistance 1 or higher when the route is unstable, flooded, occupied, watched, chemically fouled, or being crossed under time pressure. Do not make every few feet another roll. Establish one danger for a section of hollow, resolve the unit's approach to it, and allow safe movement through the section once that danger has been understood or removed.

Hollows provide strong advantages but impose physical limits:

- Far attacks and surface observation cannot pass through solid earth.
- Movement may be reduced to crawling where the gap narrows.
- Long weapons, rigid equipment, and bodies may not fit through every passage.
- Explosions and heavy movement above can change or collapse the route.
- Smoke, gas, fire, and floodwater follow airflow and slope rather than the surface map.
- A single blocked exit can turn shelter into a sealed grave.

## When the Hollow Changes

A hollow is persistent terrain, but never guaranteed terrain. Mark changes rather than erasing the route:

- A collapse seals one branch and opens a room beside it.
- Floodwater makes one descent impassable and reveals where another exit rises.
- Enemy braces and footprints show that the far end is now occupied.
- A new crater creates a dangerous entrance in exposed ground.
- A bombardment joins two cellar systems that were previously separate.
- Something below has widened a passage without leaving excavated soil.

When a known hollow changes, preserve what the unit learned about its direction, construction, and connections. Even a sealed route tells the sappers what lies beneath and where another entrance might be found.

## Revealing New Ground

When the Front exposes a structure, draw its visible footprint on the shared map immediately. Do not demand a check to notice a house rising from a crater or a street opened by shellfire. Use [[Skill Checks and Exploration#Resistance 0 Safe Analysis|Resistance 0]] when safe study can reveal additional details, and use dangerous checks only when entry, excavation, instability, enemy observation, or something inside can retaliate.

Reveal the place in layers:

1. **Outline:** What has emerged, where it sits, and how it changes movement or sight.
2. **Access:** Doors, windows, shell openings, tunnels, flooded sections, and blocked passages.
3. **Use:** Shelter, observation, salvage, routes, evidence, and positions the enemy may occupy.
4. **History:** Civilian purpose, later military uses, and signs that connect it to the wider war.
5. **Wrongness:** One contradiction, living process, borrowed memory, or impossible preservation when the operation needs horror.

> [!TIP] Draw Only What Is Known
> Help the players annotate each confirmed layer. Use room outlines, arrows, short labels, and question marks rather than drafting a complete architectural plan. If only part of a buried town has emerged, draw only that part. The blank ground around it remains a promise and a threat.

## Changing a Persistent Map

A shifting Front should complicate knowledge, not make it worthless. Preserve what the unit earned whenever possible.

- A collapsed safe route may reveal why it collapsed and expose another route.
- A destroyed shelter may become a crater, rubble wall, or entrance to the cellar beneath it.
- A flooded trench may carry bodies, equipment, and evidence to a new marked location.
- An enemy advance may occupy a known landmark without changing its layout.
- New wire may alter an approach while the unit's recorded firing lanes remain useful.
- A bombardment may erase an uncertain mark but should confirm something else through the destruction.

Cross out and revise old annotations instead of erasing them. Date or number major versions when useful. A palimpsest of corrections shows that the sappers have survived long enough to watch geography become unreliable.

Do not shift the map merely because the unit found an effective route. Give every change a cause the players could observe or later discover. Enemy adaptation should answer knowledge the enemy actually obtained. Weather and shelling should change several connected features, not select only the sappers' best advantage.

## 2d6: What the Front Uncovers

Roll two six-sided dice when bombardment, collapse, or weather opens an older layer. Results near 7 are common remains of civilian life and earlier fortification. Extremes are rare and wrong.

| 2d6 | The ground exposes |
| ---: | --- |
| 2 | A complete room untouched by dust, containing warm food and a family who insist the bombardment has not begun |
| 3 | A sealed reconstruction ward whose numbered patients share parts with living members of the unit |
| 4 | An old command gallery with maps, firing records, and a route omitted from every current chart |
| 5 | A row of burnt house fronts backed by connected cellars and one defensible courtyard |
| 6 | A workshop or storehouse containing damaged tools, useful materials, and signs of hurried evacuation |
| 7 | A civilian cellar, kitchen, shop, chapel, or schoolroom converted by several generations of soldiers |
| 8 | A buried street or rail spur that creates a faster route but passes beneath watched ground |
| 9 | A collapsed aid station containing bodies, medical supplies, and records from both armies |
| 10 | An earlier trench line with intact firing steps, blocked dugouts, and obsolete mine warnings |
| 11 | A crypt or communal shelter whose dead have been catalogued for future military use |
| 12 | The upper floor of a building rising from below, its windows looking down into earth and its occupants still moving behind them |

The result is a starting shape, not a complete encounter. Give it one immediate use, one danger, and one question the unit can pursue. If a failed check exposes it, apply [[Skill Checks and Exploration#Bought Knowledge|Bought Knowledge]] through the discovery without cancelling the retaliation.

## Referee Details

Use one or two details at a time:

- Wallpaper protected behind the outline of a removed cabinet
- Tram rails rising from mud and ending at a crater wall
- A house number repeated on three military bunkers built from the same street
- Charred floorboards polished smooth by years of boots
- A cellar window opening into the side of a trench
- Roots growing through a dining table without disturbing the place settings
- Enemy sandbags sewn from curtains carrying Allied ration marks
- A street sign used as a surgeon's drainage board
- Children's height marks continued upward in several soldiers' handwriting
- A church bell beneath the ground ringing only when artillery passes overhead
- A bathtub filled with clean water in a district without working pumps
- A front door standing alone, still locked, with fresh mud on the interior handle
