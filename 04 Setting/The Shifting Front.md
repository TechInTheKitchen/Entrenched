# The Shifting Front

The Front is called fixed because communiqués measure it from far away. At ground level it never holds still.

Trenches advance, collapse, and are dug again. Artillery moves ridgelines one crater at a time. Rain carries whole revetments downhill. A position may remain under the same designation for twenty years while every wall, approach, body, and firing step around it is replaced.

The Front also moves vertically. Thirty-seven years of shelling have buried villages beneath spoil, driven roads under mud, and stacked new defenses over old ones. Another bombardment cuts down through those layers. A cellar sealed in 1916 opens beneath a crater in 1951. A trench wall sloughs away and exposes the upper windows of a house everyone believed flattened. The battlefield does not discover new ground. It remembers what was put underneath.

## How the Ground Moves

Changes should have visible causes. The Front shifts through:

- **Bombardment:** Shells destroy cover, open craters, expose buried structures, and collapse tunnels into one another.
- **Sapper work:** Both armies excavate galleries, drainage channels, mines, countermines, and reinforcement trenches throughout the night.
- **Weather:** Rain floods low ground, carries bodies and equipment, strips soil from masonry, and fills yesterday's routes with mud.
- **Occupation:** An advance turns old enemy works into Allied positions. A retreat abandons kitchens, hospitals, depots, prisoners, and unfinished repairs.
- **Reconstruction:** Corpse routes, donor pits, Black Matter seams, and living fortifications alter soil that has absorbed too many rebuilt bodies.
- **Weight:** Tanks, guns, marching formations, and repeated detonations break roofs and streets that still exist beneath the surface.

A shift may happen during a run, between runs, or between operations. During a run, show the immediate cause and let the sappers react. Between runs, mark the change before deployment through artillery reports, aerial photographs, survivor testimony, or what can be seen from the Allied parapet.

## Buried Towns

Most towns on military maps no longer resemble settlements. Their names identify sectors, artillery coordinates, and burial grounds. The buildings survive below the violence in disconnected pieces:

- Cellars joined by shell holes and later military tunnels
- Ground floors buried until their windows become doors
- Churches reduced to towers, crypts, bells, and lines of foundation stone
- Factories whose boilers, belts, and rail spurs remain under collapsed roofs
- Schools used successively as hospitals, barracks, butcheries, and ammunition stores
- Sewers and wells that connect positions belonging to both armies
- Rows of houses preserved as scorched partitions with no exterior walls
- Civic shelters still bearing names that do not appear in military records

When one of these places emerges, retain its domestic purpose. A kitchen is more than cover. It has hooks, plates, a stove, a family tally cut into a doorframe, and thirty years of soldiers using it for something else. The horror comes from recognizing what the fortification used to be.

The people who lived there may be dead, displaced, incorporated into military institutions, or still below. Do not make every buried room contain a monster. Empty beds, carefully stored shoes, dry account books, and a hand-painted shop sign can carry the weight of the missing population without explanation.

## Burnt-Out Husks

Fire rarely removes a building cleanly. It leaves iron frames, brick shells, fused glass, carbonized beams, and rooms opened like diagrams. Later armies reinforce those remains because anything still standing has already survived something.

A burnt husk can provide:

- Artillery shelter behind thick machinery or cellar walls
- A high observation point that silhouettes anyone using it
- A route through adjoining basements or broken service tunnels
- Materials for braces, traps, fires, repairs, and field revival
- Evidence of an older occupation or atrocity
- A landmark visible from several parts of the map
- A defensible position already registered by enemy guns
- A structure whose heat, ash, or dead occupants never behaved correctly afterward

Make its value double-edged. The factory boiler stops fragments but carries voices through its pipes. The church tower gives a clear view and can be seen by every observer in the sector. The cellar offers a hidden route but contains the bodies of everyone who once trusted it.

## The Hollows Beneath the Ruins

Burnt buildings often leave more below the soil than above it. Fire consumes floors, joists, doors, and furniture while foundations, cellar arches, drains, retaining walls, and street-level masonry remain. Later shelling throws earth and rubble across the surviving structure without filling every room. The result is a chain of compressed voids beneath no-man's-land: gaps between collapsed floors, half-filled cellars, buried alleys, sewers, culverts, coal chutes, and rooms connected by holes blasted through adjoining walls.

Sappers call these routes **hollows**. A hollow may run beneath wire, pass under a registered firing lane, or connect two buildings whose visible ruins appear isolated. Some are only wide enough for a crawling body. Others contain whole streets beneath roofs made from compacted soil, wreckage, and the weight of later fortifications.

Signs of a hollow include:

- Mud sinking in a straight line where a cellar row continues below
- Warm, chemical, or sweet air breathing through cracked masonry
- Rainwater vanishing beneath rubble without forming a pool
- Boots, tracks, blood, or telephone wire disappearing into an apparent dead end
- A hollow sound beneath a rifle butt, probe, or careful footstep
- Brick dust in a fresh crater where no building remains on current maps
- Smoke or voices emerging from a drain far from their source
- A wall, stair rail, chimney, or length of pipe descending into solid earth

### What Waits Below

The earth beneath the Front has been protected from daylight, not from war.

- **Bad air:** Gas, smoke, rot, and oxygenless pockets settle in low chambers. Dead candles, tarnished metal, insects, and animal bodies should warn the unit before breathing becomes a check.
- **Water:** Flooded rooms conceal shafts, wire, bodies, and currents leading toward deeper breaks. A route may remain passable only while pumps elsewhere continue working.
- **Unstable weight:** Soil, tanks, guns, and newer trenches rest on damaged arches. Cracks, falling grit, bowed brick, and sounds from above announce a collapse before it kills.
- **Old works:** Mines, countermines, demolition charges, and listening galleries from forgotten offensives remain connected to civilian foundations. Their markings may belong to either army or neither current language.
- **Occupants:** Enemy sappers, deserters, corpse-recovery teams, and things avoiding both armies use the same protected routes. Signs of habitation should appear before contact.
- **The unremoved dead:** Cellars served as shelters, hospitals, execution rooms, and donor stores. Bodies may block a passage, reinforce a wall, or remember enough movement to follow the living.
- **Black Matter:** Seams collect below groundwater and around mass reconstruction sites. Roots, wire, teeth, and borrowed nerves grow more freely where shellfire does not burn them away.
- **Lost direction:** Sound travels through pipes and joined masonry. Compasses turn toward Black Matter. A sapper may hear the unit ahead, behind, and inside the wall at once.

The sappers may prefer the dark below because shellfire cannot reach it. The signs of what waits there should make that choice costly, never blind.


## What the Shift Means

The moving Front prevents either army from owning the ground completely. A trench can be captured, renamed, reinforced, and lost while the town beneath it continues to surface room by room. Soldiers defend ruins belonging to people their governments no longer acknowledge. Sappers navigate yesterday's enemy works using memories from bodies recovered there decades earlier.

Command treats every emergence as terrain, salvage, or intelligence. The sappers are close enough to see that it was once a place where people expected tomorrow to resemble today.
