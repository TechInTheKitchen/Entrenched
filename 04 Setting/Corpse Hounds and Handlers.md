# Corpse Hounds and Their Handlers

The Allied trench does not leave recovery entirely to soldiers. When a unit fails to return, the gates beneath the rear line open and the **corpse hounds** are led into no-man's-land.

They are not dogs, though most began with dogs somewhere in their ancestry. A corpse hound is a low, broad recovery animal rebuilt to cross wire, flooded shell holes, collapsed trenches, and ground still moving under bombardment. Its ribs are reinforced into a carrying frame. Its jaw can close around cloth, webbing, bone, or a recovery tag without crushing what it holds. Beneath layers of scar and oilcloth, its nose has been divided into wet chambers that sort blood, Black Matter, regimental chemicals, and the fading trace the Butchers call **continuity**.

Handlers use a simpler word.

**Self.**

## What the Hounds Find

Every recruit is scented before receiving a rifle. A clerk takes blood, hair, breath condensed against cold glass, and a thumbprint pressed into rendered fat. The samples are sealed in a regimental scent ledger. Before a recovery, the handler lets the hound smell the appropriate page.

A corpse hound can follow more than blood. It can distinguish one sapper's remains from the bodies packed around them, recognize a repeatedly rebuilt sapper beneath donor flesh, and track pieces separated by shellfire. The oldest hounds sometimes identify a sapper whose scent page has been destroyed.

Nobody agrees on how.

The handlers insist the animals remember the regiment. The Butchers claim continuity leaves a measurable residue in living tissue. Chaplains say the hounds smell the portion of the soul that has not yet understood it is dead.

All three explanations have led hounds to the same body.

## Recovery Harness

A working hound wears a leather-and-canvas harness fitted with:

- **Retrieval hooks** for webbing, coats, and bodies too damaged to grip safely.
- **Scent tins** containing the unit's regimental samples and articles taken from individual bunks.
- **Red thread reels** used to mark a route back through disturbed ground.
- **Brass tally tags** for each body named in the recovery order.
- **A hood and bite bar** for restraining the hound when donor memories overwhelm its training.
- **One sealed phosphorus charge** for remains that cannot be permitted to return.

The last item is carried under the handler's seal. Its presence is never discussed during the briefing.

Corpse hounds do not drag bodies like dead weight when there is time. They pull a torso onto the reinforced back, gather loose pieces into the rib harness, and carry the remains with a care that appears almost tender. When hurried, they take whatever purchase they can and run.

## The Handler

A **handler** is part tracker, part kennel keeper, part mortuary clerk. Most are former stretcher-bearers, failed Butcher apprentices, or sappers no longer able to cross the open ground at unit pace. They learn scent preparation, battlefield navigation, restraint procedures, and enough anatomy to decide which pieces must come home.

Handlers carry no rank that line infantry recognizes. In the recovery yard, however, their orders override officers. A colonel may demand that a body be brought back. Only the handler decides whether the thing at the end of the lead is still that body.

Good handlers speak to their hounds constantly. They name landmarks, read the recovery roll aloud, and describe the person being sought:

*Private Vale. Left-handed. Coal smoke in the lungs. Afraid of horses. Returned twice.*

This is partly training. It is also a precaution. Hounds exposed to too many dead memories begin answering to names that were never theirs.

Handlers are taught five standing rules:

1. **Trust the nose before the face.** Reconstruction makes appearance unreliable.
2. **Count the tags before returning.** A hound may bring back more than it was sent to find.
3. **Never follow a silent hound underground.** Make it bark first. Listen for an answer.
4. **Do not let two hounds divide the same body.** They may disagree about which pieces contain the sapper.
5. **If the hound recognizes you as dead, report to the Butchers before sleeping.**

## The Bond

A handler and hound are paired until one of them can no longer work. The hound learns the handler's gait, pulse, fear, and buried memories. The handler learns which whine means artillery, which means Black Matter, and which means the trail continues beneath the soil.

The relationship is affectionate, but never safe. A hound may protect its handler from shellfire and later attempt to open the handler's chest because it smells a familiar donor organ inside. Handlers sleep beyond the kennel bars. They place a worn glove beneath the hound's muzzle so it remembers whom not to retrieve.

When a handler dies, the hound is sent to find the body. If it returns with the correct remains, the handler may be revived and the pairing continues. If it refuses the scent, kennel doctrine records the handler as **disputed**. Disputed bodies go to a separate table in the Deep Works.

When a hound dies, its usable parts enter the same repair stores as everything else. Handlers are not permitted to request them. They frequently recognize them later.

## A Recovery in No-Man's-Land

Recovery teams usually move after bombardment, when smoke hides them and fresh craters have broken the established paths. A team consists of one handler, one or two hounds, and sometimes a sapper escort. The handler does not search the whole battlefield. They follow a sequence:

1. Give the hound the sapper's scent and show it the last confirmed position on the unit map.
2. Follow until the hound indicates **blood**, **continuity**, or **wrongness**. Each signal uses a different posture and lead tension.
3. Mark hazards and recoverable routes as the hound exposes them.
4. Confirm the remains with tags, equipment, anatomy, remembered wounds, and the hound's response.
5. Gather enough of the sapper for the Butchers to establish identity.
6. Follow the red thread home before artillery or scavengers close the route.

The hound may find the body quickly. Getting it back is the operation.

## When Recovery Goes Wrong

Corpse hounds fail in ways instruments do not:

- The hound finds several bodies carrying pieces of the same sapper.
- A buried structure contains the scent, but something inside has learned to imitate the hound's recall whistle.
- The hound refuses a recognizable corpse and leads the handler toward a living soldier.
- Enemy handlers have crossed the trail with captured Allied blood.
- The remains stand, crawl, or call the hound by its kennel name.
- The hound returns alone with every tally tag removed and one unfamiliar tag tied beneath its tongue.
- A recovered sapper wakes in the harness before reaching the trench and begs not to be taken to the Butchers.

Do not use every failed recovery to deny revival. Most hounds perform their work. Their horror comes from how routinely the trench depends on them and from the rare moment when an animal trained to recognize a person decides that person is no longer present.

## Allied, Enemy, and Feral Hounds

**Allied corpse hounds** recognize regiment scent and normally ignore living sappers unless commanded, threatened, or confused by donor tissue.

**Enemy recovery animals** perform the same military function under different handling doctrine. Some retrieve Allied bodies for study. Others have been trained to separate useful anatomy in the field.

**Feral corpse hounds** are lost, abandoned, or handlerless animals whose recovery commands never ended. They follow the dead, collect bodies, and assemble caches in dugouts or shell holes. A feral hound is not automatically hostile. It may attempt to return a fallen sapper to a trench that no longer exists.

## Referee Use

> [!TIP] Presenting a Recovery
> Use corpse hounds to make recovery visible, uncertain, and personal. The hound may find the body quickly. Getting it home should create the pressure.

- Before a run, let a handler scent the unit and ask one sapper for an article from their bunk.
- After a failed operation, describe what the hounds had to cross and what they brought back with the body.
- Let tracks, red thread, kennel whistles, and tally tags warn the unit that another recovery team is nearby.
- When a sapper is left behind, keep their location on the map and reveal the hound's approach one finding at a time.
- Let a hound recognize a heavily reconstructed sapper when friends or records do not.
- Reserve hostile hounds for broken doctrine, enemy action, or a recovery instinct turned inside out.

> [!IMPORTANT] The Handler Is an Ally
> The handler should usually be a cooperative presence. They want the sappers home, know routes command has ignored, and understand that a body may still be a person. Their limits create the horror: they cannot stop the shelling, cannot overrule a Burn order, and cannot promise that everything the hound retrieves should be revived.

>[!tip] **A Voice for the Handler**
>Speak plainly and use names. A handler has seen too much anatomy to be impressed by gore. What unsettles them is uncertainty: a scent that divides, a body that remembers the wrong kennel, or a hound wagging its tail for someone officially dead.
